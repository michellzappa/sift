# Sift: why it exists

Working draft of the human value proposition. Not final copy.

Lead: insight. Sift shows you what you actually care about. Fewer tabs is
the side effect, not the pitch.

## One-liners to choose from

- Your open tabs are a map of what you care about. Sift reads it back to you.
- Your interests sprawl. That is fine. Now they have a shape.
- See what you keep coming back to.
- Keep your curiosity. Lose the tabs. (relief line, for later in the page)

## What you see

Point Sift at everything you have kept: your open tabs on every device and
your Reading List. Your own Claude reads it and tells you what you keep
circling around. Not the folders you meant to make. The themes you actually
live in.

Some of it you expected. Some of it you did not. A theme you thought was a
passing thing turns out to have forty links behind it. A topic you call work
has two. Your attention leaves a trail, and this is the first time you see
it drawn.

Run it again in a month and you see what grew, what faded and what you keep
saving but never touch.

## The problem, in human terms

You have four hundred tabs open on your phone. You are not disorganized. You
are curious about a lot of things, and every one of those tabs is a small
promise to yourself: this could matter later.

The trouble is that the browser keeps the link and forgets the promise. A week
later you cannot tell the article you meant to cite from the one you opened by
accident. So you keep all of them, and the pile becomes a low hum of guilt
every time you open Safari.

Every tool for this asks you to do the hard part first. Folders want you to
decide where something goes before you know what it is. Read-later apps want
you to come back and read. Note apps want you to write. None of them
admit that most of what you save is half a thought, and that the honest
answer for a lot of it is "not now, maybe never".

## What Sift does instead

Sift reads everything you have kept: your open tabs on every device and your
Reading List. Then your own Claude does the tedious part. It reads each link,
works out what you were circling around, and names the themes you keep
coming back to.

You get your interests back as a picture. Some themes are clearly work. Some
are clearly not, and seeing forty saved links about one hobby is its own kind
of information. A theme with thirty links and nowhere to go is a question
worth asking: is this a project yet?

Then each link gets one of a few honest next steps. Read it properly. Try the
tool. Quote it somewhere. Contact the person. File it for later. Or let it go.
Once a link has a next step, it can leave the browser.

## How it should feel

Like a friend who read everything on your desk and says, "you have three
things going on here, and one of them you keep pretending is not a thing."

Not like a filing cabinet. You never sort anything by hand unless you want to.
You only say yes or no to what Claude proposes.

Closing four hundred tabs should feel like relief, not like loss. Nothing you
cared about disappears. It just moves to where you actually work.

## What it is not

Sift is not another second brain. It does not want to be where your
knowledge lives. It is the step between "I saved this" and "this is in the
right place", and then it gets out of the way. Notes, documents and tasks
stay in the tools you already use.

It is also not a productivity scolding. Sprawling interests are not a problem
to fix. Sift assumes your curiosity is the point and tries to make it
cheaper to follow.

## Trust

- It runs on your Mac and reads Safari's files without changing them.
- It uses your own Claude: your Claude Code login or your own API key. No
  Sift account, no Sift server.
- It sends Claude titles, links and short previews. It never sends whole pages.
- You approve every theme and every move. Nothing is filed behind your back.
- The code is open.

## Who it is for

People whose browser is a record of their curiosity: researchers, founders,
writers, designers, anyone who follows threads across work and life and has
stopped pretending they will "get to all of these this weekend".

## A number that tells the story

In one real test, 520 saved links came out as 20 themes. 76 of those links
were noise that could go at once. The rest each had a home or a next step.
The person had been carrying all 520 around for most of a year.
